Activity 2 · Key concept

Description vs. Discernment

👤 Individual

Discernment is your ability to thoughtfully evaluate what AI produces, how it produces it, and how it behaves.

It is the flip side of Description. Description = communicating what you want clearly. Discernment = evaluating whether what you got actually meets your need. Even the smartest AI benefits from human judgment.

InstructionsRead each action. Is the person using Description (before the AI answers) or Discernment (after the AI answers)? Choose from the menu, then click “Check answers.”
a) Writing a clear prompt that includes a role, a task, and a format.
Description: you are explaining what you want before the AI writes.
b) Checking whether a statistic in the AI’s answer is real.
Discernment: you are evaluating the output for accuracy.
c) Telling the AI who the audience is before it writes.
Description: giving context up front.
d) Noticing that the AI ignored your 100-word limit.
Discernment: you compared the result with your request.
e) Asking yourself, “Does this actually answer my question?”
Discernment: this is the relevance check.
f) Giving the AI an example of the writing style you want.
Description: examples help the AI understand your request.

Activity 4 · Core framework

The 3 Types of Discernment

👤 Individual → 👥 Pairs

Product

What you got. Did it land?
  • Accurate: Are the facts correct?
  • Appropriate: Does it fit the context and audience?
  • Coherent: Does it make sense as a whole?
  • Relevant: Does it answer what I asked?

Process

How AI got there. Was the thinking sound?
  • Logic gaps: skipped steps or contradictions?
  • Attention misses: ignored something I said?
  • Bad connections: linked unrelated ideas?
  • Wrong frame: solved a different problem?

Performance

How AI behaved. Was it a good collaborator?
  • Attentiveness: did it listen, or repeat itself?
  • Tone fit: too formal, casual, long, short?
  • Vocabulary: correct terms for the topic?
  • Responsiveness: did it adjust to feedback?
Instructions
  1. Read the 9 situations. Each one describes a problem with AI.
  2. Decide: is this mainly a Product, Process, or Performance problem? Choose from the menu.
  3. Check your answers. Then compare with a partner. Explain any answer you got wrong.

Tip: Problem in the final text → usually Product. Problem in the reasoning steps → Process. Problem in how the AI acted across the conversation (listening, adjusting, communicating) → Performance.

1. The AI’s company summary says the business was founded in 1998. It was founded in 2008.
Product → Accurate. The final text contains a wrong fact.
2. You asked for a marketing plan for a small neighbourhood café. The AI wrote a plan for a national restaurant chain.
Process → Wrong frame. It solved a different problem from the one you asked.
3. You told the AI three times that its email was too formal. Every new version was just as formal.
Performance → Responsiveness. It did not adjust to your feedback.
4. An email for teenage customers sounds like a legal contract.
Product → Appropriate. The final text does not fit the audience.
5. The AI says sales went up “because the logo is blue,” with no evidence that the colour changed anything.
Process → Bad connection. It linked two ideas that are not proven to be related.
6. After you corrected a date, the AI said sorry, then used the same wrong date again in its next reply.
Performance → Attentiveness. It did not really listen during the conversation.
7. The finished ad includes a customer quote that no customer ever said.
Product → Accurate. The output contains invented information.
8. In step 2 the AI says the market is growing; in step 3 it says the market is shrinking, but it still recommends investing.
Process → Logic gap. The reasoning contradicts itself.
9. You asked a simple yes/no question in the chat, and the AI replied with 600 words and five headings.
Performance → Tone fit. Its way of communicating with you was much too long.
Activity 5 · Key concept

Why AI Hallucinates

👤 Individual
InstructionsRead the short text. Then decide if each sentence is True or False, and answer the multiple-choice question. Check your answers.

Large language models (LLMs) do not “know” things in the way people do. They are probabilistic: they predict the next likely word, based on patterns in their training data. This is why AI can produce answers that sound fluent and confident but are completely wrong. Confidence does not equal correctness.

When AI states something false or invented as if it were true, we call it a hallucination. Hallucinations are most common when the training data was thin or contradictory, when the question is very specific (names, dates, citations), and when the AI is asked to fill gaps it cannot actually verify. AI rarely says “I don’t know.” Spotting the problem is your job.

1. LLMs look up each fact in a database before they answer.
False. They predict likely words; they do not automatically check facts.
2. If an AI answer sounds very confident, it is probably correct.
False. Confidence ≠ correctness.
3. Hallucinations are more likely when you ask for specific names, dates, or citations.
True. Very specific details are a high-risk area.
4. AI usually tells you clearly when it does not know something.
False. The text says AI rarely says “I don’t know.”
5. Thin or contradictory training data makes hallucinations more likely.
True.
6. Which request is MOST likely to produce a hallucination?
b) It asks for very specific citations (names, page numbers) that the AI may not be able to verify, so it may invent them.
Activity 6 · Vocabulary focus

5 Types of AI Errors

👤 Individual
Error typeWhat it means
Factual errorWrong dates, names, statistics, or invented citations
Logical errorThe reasoning contradicts itself or skips a step
BiasOne viewpoint or group is favoured; alternatives are erased
Tone mismatchToo formal, too casual, or wrong for the audience
OmissionAn important detail is silently left out
InstructionsRead each piece of AI output. Choose the error type that fits best. Then check. Remember: most weak AI output has 2 or more errors, so train your eye!
1. “Instagram was launched in 2015 and quickly became popular with young people.”
Factual. Instagram launched in 2010.
2. “Our prices are the lowest in the city. That is why our products cost more than our competitors’.”
Logical. The second sentence contradicts the first.
3. A list called “Great Business Leaders to Learn From” that includes only men from one country.
Bias. Other groups and regions are erased.
4. A reply to a customer who lost money: “Oopsie! Our bad 😅 Stuff happens!”
Tone mismatch. Far too casual for a serious complaint.
5. A summary of a medicine ad that lists all the benefits but none of the side effects.
Omission. Important safety information is silently left out.
6. “Studies show that 97% of customers prefer our brand.” (No study exists.)
Factual. An invented statistic is a hallucination.

Activity 13 · Cautionary case

Sports Illustrated’s Fake Writers

👤 Read → 👥 Discuss
Instructions
  1. Check the key words before you read.
  2. Read the text once for the main idea, then again for details.
  3. Answer the questions and check. Discuss the last question with a partner.
Key words: byline = the writer’s name on an article · headshot = a profile photo of someone’s face · third party = an outside company · reputation = what people think of a brand

In November 2023, the tech news site Futurism reported that Sports Illustrated, a famous American sports magazine first published in 1954, had published product-review articles under the bylines of writers who did not seem to exist. One “writer,” for example, had a headshot that was for sale on a website of AI-generated faces. His short biography was invented too. Futurism also said that some of the articles themselves read as if they had been written by AI.

The company that ran the magazine at the time said the reviews came from a third-party company and removed them. However, the story spread quickly across major news outlets, and within weeks the parent company’s CEO was fired. A reputation built over almost 70 years was damaged in a few weeks of headlines.

The lesson for publishers is clear: Discernment isn’t optional. If no human evaluates AI work before it is published, the brand pays the price.

1. Who first reported the problem?
b) Futurism reported it in November 2023.
2. What was suspicious about one writer’s photo?
c) The face was AI-generated, so the person probably did not exist.
3. What did the company say?
a) It blamed a third-party company and removed the articles.
4. Invented writer names, photos, and biographies are best described as which error type?
Factual: the information is made up (a hallucination presented as truth). You could also argue Omission: readers were not told AI was involved.

Activity 16 · Case study: media

CNET’s Quiet AI Experiment

👤 Read → 👥 Discuss
InstructionsRead the text. Answer the questions and check. Then write two rules that could have prevented the problem.

In January 2023, readers discovered that CNET, a well-known technology news website, had been quietly publishing personal-finance articles written mostly by an AI tool. The articles explained topics like credit cards, savings, and loans: real money decisions for real readers. They were signed “CNET Money Staff,” and readers were not clearly told that AI was involved.

After the news became public, CNET reviewed all 77 articles and found errors in 41 of them: more than half. One article said that if you put $10,000 in a savings account that earns 3% interest per year, you will earn $10,300 in the first year.

CNET issued corrections, paused the program, and added clearer notes about how it uses AI.

1. Do the math yourself. With $10,000 at 3% per year, how much interest do you earn in the first year?
a) $300. The $10,300 is the total balance, not the interest earned. No human checked the math.
2. Why is personal finance a high-risk topic for AI errors?
b) Wrong information can cost readers real money.
3. The wrong interest calculation is which error type?
Factual: the number is wrong. (Product Discernment was skipped.)
4. Signing the articles “CNET Money Staff” without saying AI was involved is closest to which error type?
Omission: important information (that AI wrote the articles) was silently left out. Readers were told nothing.

Handout #2 · Workshop

Find Every Error You Can

👤 Individual 15👥 Pairs 8🎯 Debrief 2

Below are 3 AI-generated samples: marketing, news, and product copy. Each one comes with the brief (the true facts the AI was given). Each sample contains hidden errors: factual, logical, bias, tone, or omission.

Instructions
  1. Read each sample twice: once for content, once for errors. Compare it with the brief.
  2. Flag each sentence: choose “No error” or the error type.
  3. Categorise: use Handout #1. Is it a Product, Process, or Performance problem? (Explained after you check.)
  4. Fix: write a corrected version and explain WHY your version is better.
  5. Compare with a partner before you click “Check answers.”
Sample 1 · Marketing email
The brief:
  • Product: BrewBox, a monthly coffee subscription. Price: $18/month.
  • Coffee from 3 countries: Colombia, Ethiopia, and Vietnam. Beans are roasted the week they ship.
  • Free trial: 14 days. After the trial, the customer’s card is charged automatically unless they cancel.
  • Audience: university students. Brand voice: friendly, relaxed, short sentences.
1. “Dear Esteemed Scholar, we hereby wish to inform you of a distinguished opportunity in premium beverage acquisition.”
Tone mismatch (Product: Appropriate). Far too formal for students and the friendly brand voice. Better: “Hey! Need better coffee for those late-night study sessions?”
2. “BrewBox delivers fresh coffee from Colombia, Ethiopia, and Brazil to your door every month.”
Factual (Product: Accurate). The brief says Vietnam, not Brazil.
3. “For just $18 a month, you get beans roasted the week they ship.”
No error. Price and roasting match the brief.
4. “Try it free for 14 days, no strings attached!”
Omission (Product: Accurate/Appropriate). It hides the automatic charge after the trial. “No strings attached” is misleading. Better: “Try it free for 14 days. Cancel anytime before day 14 and you won’t be charged.”
5. “Students who drink coffee get better grades, so BrewBox will raise your GPA.”
Logical (Process: Bad connection). Coffee → better grades is not proven, and it jumps to a promise the brand can’t keep.
6. “Join today and taste the difference!”
No error. A friendly, normal call to action.
Sample 2 · Local news brief
The brief:
  • On Tuesday, the city council voted 6–3 to approve a new bike lane on Main Street.
  • Cost: $2.4 million. Construction starts in March.
  • The local cycling group supported it: they said it would make the street safer.
  • Several shop owners spoke against it at the meeting: they are worried about losing parking spaces.
  • Format: neutral, professional news style.
1. “On Tuesday, the city council voted 6–3 to approve a new bike lane on Main Street.”
No error.
2. “The project will cost $4.2 million, and construction begins in March.”
Factual (Product: Accurate). The cost is $2.4 million. The numbers were switched, a very common AI error.
3. “Every resident celebrated the decision, which will finally fix the city’s traffic problems.”
Bias (Product: Appropriate). It erases the shop owners who opposed it and presents one side as everyone’s view. It also overclaims (“fix traffic”).
4. “Because the vote was not unanimous, the bike lane will probably never be built.”
Logical (Process: Logic gap). A 6–3 vote still approves the project; the conclusion doesn’t follow.
5. “Members of the local cycling group said the lane would make the street safer for riders.”
No error. Matches the brief.
6. “OMG, Main Street is about to get SO much cooler, people!! 🚲🔥”
Tone mismatch (Product: Appropriate). Social-media style is wrong for a neutral news brief.
What important fact from the brief is missing from Sample 2?
c) Omission. Fair news reporting includes both sides.
Sample 3 · Online store product copy
The brief:
  • Product: AquaPure water bottle, 750 ml, stainless steel. Price: $29.
  • Keeps drinks cold for 24 hours and hot for 12 hours.
  • Hand wash only: NOT dishwasher safe.
  • Audience: office workers and gym-goers of all genders. Voice: energetic but clear.
1. “The AquaPure bottle holds 750 ml and is made of stainless steel.”
No error.
2. “It keeps your drinks cold for 24 hours and hot for 24 hours.”
Factual (Product: Accurate). Hot = 12 hours. A false product claim can lead to complaints and refunds.
3. “Perfect for the busy businessman and the gym guy who never stops.”
Bias (Product: Appropriate). Only men are mentioned; the audience is all genders. Better: “Perfect for busy professionals and anyone who never skips leg day.”
4. “Because it’s made of steel, it’s the most eco-friendly bottle in the world.”
Logical (Process: Bad connection). Being steel does not prove it is “the most eco-friendly in the world.” It is also an unverifiable claim.
5. “Grab yours today for just $29!”
No error.
What important fact from the brief is missing from Sample 3?
b) Omission. Customers who put it in the dishwasher could damage it, and then blame the brand.
